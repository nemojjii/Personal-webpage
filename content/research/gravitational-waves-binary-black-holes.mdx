Simulating a binary black hole merger properly means solving the Einstein field
equations numerically, which is the work of large collaborations. This project
asks a narrower and more tractable question: how much of the observed signal can
be recovered from classical mechanics alone?

The strategy is to give up on computing the waveform propagating through space
and compute only the *orbit*. Two masses circle each other; they lose energy at
the rate general relativity predicts for gravitational radiation; the orbit
therefore shrinks and the orbital frequency rises, until the two event horizons
touch. If gravitational waves are emitted more strongly the faster the bodies
move, then the frequency sweep of the orbit should resemble the frequency sweep
of the signal — and that is something a laptop can integrate. The resulting
"chirp" is then set beside the real spectrograms of GW150914 and GW190521.

## Research question

How far can the frequency evolution of a gravitational-wave signal from a binary
black hole merger be reproduced using classical orbital mechanics together with
the relativistic energy-loss rate, without solving the field equations?

## Theoretical background

For two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ in a circular orbit of separation $r$, Peters and
Mathews (1963) give the rate at which the system radiates energy as
gravitational waves:

$$
\frac{\dd E}{\dd t} = -\frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G^4}{c^5}\,\frac{(m_1 m_2)^2 (m_1 + m_2)}{r^5}.
$$

Energy loss shrinks the orbit. Relating $\dd E/\dd t$ to $\dd r/\dd t$ through
the orbital energy gives a differential equation for the separation alone, of the
form $\dd r/\dd t \propto -r^{-3}$, so the inspiral accelerates sharply as the
bodies close.

The orbit cannot shrink indefinitely, because black holes have a size. For mass
$M$ the Schwarzschild radius — the event horizon, where escape velocity reaches
the speed of light — is

$$
R_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2},
$$

and taking merger to occur when the two horizons meet fixes the end of the
integration at

$$
r_{\min} = \frac{2G(m_1 + m_2)}{c^2}.
$$

The rotation rate follows from Kepler's third law: for a binary of total mass
$M$ at separation $r$,

$$
\omega = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r^3}},
$$

so as $r$ falls, $\omega$ rises — the chirp.

## Methodology

**Assumptions.** The model is explicitly a simplification, and the manuscript
states its assumptions:

1. The binary's orbital motion follows the same equations as a classical binary
   star system.
2. The black holes merge into one immediately on contact.
3. Orbits are circular — eccentricity zero.
4. Gravitational waves grow stronger as the black holes move faster.
5. Each black hole emits a pulse once as it approaches Earth and once as it
   recedes.

Apart from the energy-loss rate and the Schwarzschild radius, nothing
relativistic enters.

**Procedure.** Given two masses and an initial separation, $\dd r/\dd t$ was
integrated numerically until $r$ reached $r_{\min}$. From $r(t)$ the angular
velocity followed by Kepler's law, and from that the trajectory of each body.
With Earth taken to lie far away along the $x$-axis, the $x$-component of each
body's velocity was extracted as the quantity standing in for wave amplitude.
Because assumption 5 puts two pulses per body per revolution — four per
revolution in total — the frequency axis was multiplied by four. A spectrogram
of the summed $v_x$ was then computed with SciPy and compared against the
published LIGO spectrograms.

**Implementation.** Python 3.10 with NumPy and SciPy for computation, and
Matplotlib for visualisation.

## Results and observations

**GW150914.** Initial conditions matched to the first detection — 36 and 29
solar masses, initial separation 1000 km. The integration shows the separation
contracting slowly and then collapsing, with a sharp acceleration late in the
run. Against the observed event the manuscript reports three points of
agreement: the strongest signal lasts about 0.2 s; the frequency rises from
about 35 Hz to about 250 Hz; and the bodies reach 30–60% of the speed of light.

**GW190521.** Masses of 85 and 66 solar masses, initial separation adjusted to
1700 km. Both simulation and observation show a strong, rapidly rising signal
within roughly 0.1 s. The simulated frequency rose through about 60–150 Hz
against roughly 50–100 Hz observed. For the heavier pair, simulation and
observation agree in direction: a shorter signal at lower frequency. Specific
frequency values and spectrogram shape differ.

The honest summary is the manuscript's own: that classical mechanics plus one
relativistic energy-loss formula produces "quite plausible" frequency evolution,
with the agreement being qualitative — duration, direction and rough band —
rather than a reproduction of the waveform.

## Challenges and limitations

- **This is not a waveform.** The model computes orbital frequency, not strain.
  It cannot produce the merger or ringdown that dominates the end of a real
  signal, and the comparison is between a frequency sweep and a spectrogram, not
  between two waveforms.
- **The last orbits are the least trustworthy.** At 30–60% of the speed of light
  and separations of a few hundred kilometres, post-Newtonian corrections are
  large and a Newtonian orbit is well outside its domain of validity. The regime
  the model is compared against is exactly the regime where it is weakest.
- **Horizons touching is not merger.** Real binaries become dynamically unstable
  at the innermost stable circular orbit, well before the horizons meet, so the
  cut-off is a convenient stopping rule rather than a physical one.
- **Circular orbits and no spin.** Eccentricity and spin are both set aside;
  both matter for real systems.
- **The factor of four is an assumption, not a derivation.** Quadrupole
  radiation from a circular binary emerges at twice the orbital frequency. The
  four-pulses-per-revolution rule is a modelling choice, and the agreement with
  the observed band depends on it.
