A network of agents that can only see their immediate neighbours is asked to
agree on a single number. Nobody has a global view, there is no leader, and the
only thing each agent can do is move a little towards the people it can hear.
Whether that is enough — and how long it takes — turns out not to be a question
about the agents at all. It is a question about the shape of the network.

This project works that claim out concretely. Each network is written down as
three matrices, the consensus rule is written as one differential equation, and
the equation is integrated numerically on graphs ranging from four nodes to a
hundred. What emerges is that a single number extracted from the network's
structure — the second-smallest eigenvalue of its Laplacian — predicts both
whether agreement happens and how quickly.

## Research question

How do network topology, connection strength and the pattern of information
flow determine the speed and stability with which a set of interacting agents
reaches consensus?

## Theoretical background

A network of $n$ agents is an undirected, unweighted graph. Its **adjacency
matrix** $A$ records who can hear whom,

$$
A_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i \text{ and } j \text{ are connected},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}
$$

and the **degree matrix** $D$ is the diagonal matrix of connection counts,
$D_{ii} = \sum_j A_{ij}$. The object that does the real work is their
difference, the **graph Laplacian**

$$
L = D - A .
$$

Each agent holds a state $x_i(t)$ and repeatedly moves towards its neighbours
by the sum of its disagreements with them,

$$
\dot{x}_i = \sum_{j \in N(i)} (x_j - x_i),
$$

which for the whole system collapses into a single linear equation,

$$
\frac{\dd \mathbf{x}}{\dd t} = -L\,\mathbf{x}.
$$

Because every row of $L$ sums to zero, the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$ is
always an eigenvector with eigenvalue $0$: a state in which everyone already
agrees never changes. The rest of the spectrum decides what happens on the way
there. $L$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so its eigenvalues are real
and non-negative,

$$
0 = \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots \le \lambda_n ,
$$

and the multiplicity of $0$ counts the connected components of the graph. The
second-smallest, $\lambda_2$, is Fiedler's **algebraic connectivity**. It is
strictly positive exactly when the graph is connected, and it sets the slowest
rate at which disagreement decays. For a disconnected graph $\lambda_2 = 0$, so
algebraic connectivity is not the same thing as the smallest *non-zero*
eigenvalue; the two coincide only when the graph is connected. The manuscript
uses the latter phrase as a synonym. This page keeps the distinction, because
the disconnected case below is exactly where it matters.

<Aside>
  This is why the question stops being about the agents. Everything that matters
  — whether agreement is reachable at all, and how fast — is already contained in
  the spectrum of a matrix built from nothing but the wiring.
</Aside>

## Methodology

**Network construction.** Networks were written as undirected graphs with each
node an agent and each edge a communication link, and were varied systematically
from sparse to dense. The worked example is a six-node graph given by the edge
list

$$
s = [\,1\;1\;2\;2\;3\;2\;4\;4\;5\,], \qquad t = [\,2\;3\;3\;4\;5\;5\;5\;6\;6\,],
$$

that is, the nine edges

$$
\begin{gathered}
(1,2),\ (1,3),\ (2,3),\ (2,4),\ (3,5),\\
(2,5),\ (4,5),\ (4,6),\ (5,6).
\end{gathered}
$$

**Matrix formulation.** For every structure, $A$, $D$ and $L = D - A$ were
constructed explicitly. For the six-node graph above the degree sequence is
$(2, 4, 3, 3, 4, 2)$, giving

$$
L =
\begin{pmatrix}
\;\;2 & -1 & -1 & \;\;0 & \;\;0 & \;\;0\\
-1 & \;\;4 & -1 & -1 & -1 & \;\;0\\
-1 & -1 & \;\;3 & \;\;0 & -1 & \;\;0\\
\;\;0 & -1 & \;\;0 & \;\;3 & -1 & -1\\
\;\;0 & -1 & -1 & -1 & \;\;4 & -1\\
\;\;0 & \;\;0 & \;\;0 & -1 & -1 & \;\;2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The four-node case analysed in detail is the subgraph induced on nodes $1$–$4$,
with edges $(1,2), (1,3), (2,3), (2,4)$, degree sequence $(2, 3, 2, 1)$ and

$$
L_4 =
\begin{pmatrix}
\;\;2 & -1 & -1 & \;\;0\\
-1 & \;\;3 & -1 & -1\\
-1 & -1 & \;\;2 & \;\;0\\
\;\;0 & -1 & \;\;0 & \;\;1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

One point of record about this case. The manuscript's printed code starts it
from $\mathbf{x}(0) = (1, 2, 3, 0)$, while its printed figure for the same graph
starts near $(10, 20, 5, 15)$ and settles at $12.5$; the two appear to have
been generated from different initial states. The demonstration below follows
the printed code.

**Numerical implementation.** The consensus equation was integrated in Python
with `networkx`, `numpy` and `matplotlib`, using the explicit Euler method,

$$
\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \Delta t\, L\, \mathbf{x}_k ,
$$

with $\Delta t = 0.1$. The four- and six-node runs printed in the manuscript
take $50$ steps from assorted initial states; the $30$- and $100$-node figures
run longer — their axes reach about $70$ and $150$ steps — and no code, step
count or initial state is printed for them. Graphs were drawn with
force-directed layouts; node trajectories were plotted against time; and the
Laplacian spectrum was examined with attention to $\lambda_2$.

**Cases simulated.** A four-node graph; a sparse six-node chain $1$–$2$–$3$–$4$–$5$
with node $6$ attached to nothing; the nine-edge six-node graph above; the
complete graph $K_6$; and sparse networks of $30$ and $100$ nodes.

## Results and observations

**Connected graphs reach agreement; disconnected ones do not.** In every
connected case the node states converged to a common value. In the sparse case
the chain $1$–$2$–$3$–$4$–$5$ converged while node $6$, having no neighbours,
stayed at its initial value for all time — its row of $L$ is identically zero,
so the dynamics never touch it.

**Density accelerates convergence.** The complete graph settled fastest and most
smoothly; the nine-edge graph next; the chain slowest. The accompanying poster
reports that convergence sped up with algebraic connectivity in the moderately
connected networks and, across the topologies tested, tracked $\lambda_2$; the
manuscript itself states the dependence as a property of the model and prints
no spectrum. With four small graphs this is an illustration of the theorem
rather than an empirical test of it, and it does not by itself establish a
universal relationship.

**Scale slows it down.** The $30$- and $100$-node sparse networks still
converged, but more slowly and with more variation than the small or dense
cases.

The table below gathers the spectra of the graphs whose matrices the manuscript
writes out, computed from those matrices:

| Graph | Edges | $\lambda_2$ | Zero eigenvalues | Behaviour reported |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chain of 5, node 6 isolated | 4 | 0 | 2 | Chain agrees, node 6 never moves |
| Four-node subgraph | 4 | 1.000 | 1 | Converges |
| Six nodes, nine edges | 9 | 1.186 | 1 | Converges, faster |
| Complete graph $K_6$ | 15 | 6.000 | 1 | Converges fastest |

The disconnected case is the clean one. Its Laplacian has **two** zero
eigenvalues rather than one, so $\lambda_2 = 0$ — and $\lambda_2 = 0$ is
precisely the algebraic statement that the graph falls into more than one piece.
The isolated node is not a numerical nuisance; it is the spectrum reporting a
fact about the topology.

## Challenges and limitations

- The model is the simplest one available: undirected, unweighted, static
  graphs, with identical agents, no delay, no noise and no external input. Real
  social and infrastructural networks are directed, weighted and time-varying.
- Convergence *rate* is governed by $\lambda_2$, but the apparent speed in a
  plot also depends on how far apart the initial states were. The cases shown
  use different initial vectors as well as different graphs, so visual
  comparisons between plots conflate the two.
- Explicit Euler is only conditionally stable, requiring $\Delta t < 2/\lambda_n$.
  It holds comfortably for every graph above — $K_6$ is the tightest at
  $\Delta t < 1/3$ — but it is a constraint the method inherits, not a property
  of consensus.
- The manuscript's applied claims about social media, public-health messaging
  and smart grids are motivation drawn from the literature, not results of these
  simulations. Nothing here was tested against real network data.
- The $30$- and $100$-node networks are described qualitatively; their edge
  lists and initial states are not recorded in the manuscript, so those two
  results cannot be reproduced from it as written.
