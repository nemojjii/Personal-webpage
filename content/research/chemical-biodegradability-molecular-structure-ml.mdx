Whether a chemical breaks down in the environment or stays there for decades is,
in the end, a fact about its molecular structure. The practical difficulty is
that establishing it experimentally is slow and expensive: a ready
biodegradability assay runs for 28 days per compound. If structure determines
fate, then descriptors computed from structure alone ought to carry enough
information to predict that fate in advance — which is the premise of
Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship modelling.

This project tests that premise on 1055 chemicals. Each is described by 41
numerical descriptors — electronic, topological and steric properties derived
from the molecular graph — and labelled by experiment as readily biodegradable
or not. The work is a classification problem, but the more interesting part is
the exploratory analysis that precedes it: finding out which descriptors are
carrying real information and which are simply restating each other.

## Research question

Can molecular descriptors computed from structure alone classify a chemical as
readily or not readily biodegradable, and which descriptors carry that
information?

## Theoretical background

A QSAR model is a mapping from the structural and physicochemical properties of
a compound to a property or activity of interest. Developing one follows a fixed
sequence: assemble compounds with known outcomes, compute molecular descriptors,
fit a statistical or machine-learning model relating descriptors to outcome,
validate it on data held out from fitting, interpret which features matter, and
only then apply it to new molecules.

The outcome here is binary. Under the OECD convention a substance counts as
**readily biodegradable (RB)** when it reaches roughly 60% biodegradation within
28 days; anything slower or requiring special conditions is **not readily
biodegradable (NRB)**. So the task is binary classification: given a descriptor
vector $\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^{41}$, predict a label in
$\{\mathrm{RB}, \mathrm{NRB}\}$.

The descriptors themselves are a mixture of counts and graph-spectral
quantities — the number of heavy atoms, the percentage of carbon atoms, the
frequency of C–N pairs at a given topological distance, alongside eigenvalue
quantities such as `SpMax_L` (leading eigenvalue of the Laplace matrix) and
`SM6_L` (spectral moment of order 6). Several are therefore different functions
of the same molecular graph, which matters when their correlations are examined.

## Methodology

**Data.** Experimental biodegradation values for 1055 chemicals were taken from
the *QSAR biodegradation* dataset in the UC Irvine Machine Learning Repository:
41 molecular descriptors and one experimental class per compound.

**Exploratory data analysis.** Descriptive statistics — minimum, maximum, mean
and standard deviation — were computed for every variable. Univariate
histograms were examined across all 41 descriptors and the class variable,
showing a mixture of approximately normal, right-skewed, multimodal and sparse
distributions. A correlation matrix was computed over all descriptors and then
over a focused subset of 15, and pairwise relationships were visualised with
`seaborn` pair plots and joint regression plots, separated by class.

**Modelling.** The data were partitioned 60% training, 20% validation and 20%
testing. Five classifiers were then fitted and compared: logistic regression, a
decision tree, a support vector machine, a random forest, and k-nearest
neighbours.

**Tools.** Python for the modelling and plotting, with Google Sheets and Excel
used alongside for tabulation.

## Results and observations

**Descriptor correlations.** The strongest relationship found was between
`SpMax_A` and `SM6_L` at $r = 0.9226$ — the leading eigenvalue of the adjacency
matrix against the sixth spectral moment of the Laplace matrix, which is to say
two summaries of the same graph spectrum tracking each other closely. A further
strong pair was `SM6_B(m)` and `SpMax_B(m)` at $r = 0.9174$, again two
functions of one Burden matrix. The most negative relationship reported was
between `SpPosA_B(p)` and `Mi` at $r = -0.7870$.

**Classification.** Of the five models tested, the support vector machine was
the most accurate at **86.7%**, with random forest close behind at **86.3%**.

| Model | Reported accuracy |
| --- | --- |
| Support vector machine | 86.7% |
| Random forest | 86.3% |
| k-nearest neighbours | 85.4% |
| Logistic regression | 85.4% |
| Decision tree | not reported individually |

The manuscript's overall conclusion is a methodological one rather than a
performance claim: that a QSAR model's usefulness depends on the quality of the
training data and on whether the chosen descriptors actually capture the
chemistry that governs the outcome.

## Challenges and limitations

- **Redundant descriptors.** The highest correlations are between pairs that are
  different spectral summaries of the same molecular graph. Highly collinear
  features inflate apparent information and destabilise coefficient estimates in
  linear models.
- **Accuracy alone is the wrong measure.** Only accuracy is reported. On a task
  where the two classes are not balanced, accuracy can be high while the
  minority class is badly handled; precision, recall, F1 and a confusion matrix
  per class would be needed to know whether the models are useful.
- **A validation split that is not used.** Twenty percent of the data was set
  aside for validation, but no hyperparameter selection or model-selection
  procedure using it is described.
- **No feature selection was carried out.** The correlation analysis identifies
  redundancy but the models appear to have been fitted on the full descriptor
  set regardless.
- **Dataset-bounded conclusions.** Every result is a statement about this one
  curated dataset. Generalisation to chemicals outside its applicability domain
  is not established, and the manuscript notes the OECD compound data as a
  larger resource not yet used.
