A proof is a strange kind of sentence. It can be entirely correct and still tell
you nothing about why the thing it proves is true. You follow each step, you
agree with each step, and at the end you have agreement without comprehension —
the conclusion has been forced on you rather than shown to you.[^1]

[^1]: This is the ordinary experience of reading a verification by case
analysis: nothing is wrong, and nothing is learned.

## Two things we want at once

We ask proofs to do two jobs that have little to do with each other. The first
is adjudication: settling whether a claim holds. The second is illumination:
making the claim seem inevitable. A proof by contradiction is often excellent at
the first and almost useless at the second. It establishes that the negation
cannot stand, and leaves the positive fact as unmotivated as it found it.

> One can prove a theorem and still not know it.

## The silence

What a proof declines to tell you is where it came from. The published argument
is the fair copy; the reasoning that produced it has been erased, and the erasure
is deliberate — the discipline requires the final text to be checkable rather
than confessional.[^2]

[^2]: Rota's essays are unusually honest about this, which is why
mathematicians keep rereading them and keep not writing like them.

The consequence is that mathematics transmits its results far better than it
transmits its reasons. Everything checkable survives; everything merely
persuasive is left in the drafts.

## Why it matters here

If the argument above is right, then understanding is not a by-product of rigour
but a separate thing that has to be written on purpose. Which is most of the
reason this site has a notebook as well as a research section: the notebook is
where the reasons go.
